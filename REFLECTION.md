## Q1. Who are your users, and what changes for them?

My primary users are external customers looking for a cafe with available reservations for the day. Without the product, they would need to check cafes individually to find out whether tables are still available. My product changes this by putting multiple cafe outlets and their reservation status in one interface, allowing the user to first identify cafes with availability and then click into a specific cafe to view its available time slots. The key change is therefore replacing the need to check cafes one by one with a single view that helps users narrow down their choices before looking at the detailed reservation availability.

## Q2. Augmented capacity and constrained capacity

The AI pairing augmented my capacity by allowing me to turn an idea for a cafe reservation tracker into a working React application without having to write the code myself. This meant that more of my time could be spent deciding what users should see, such as cafe availability, reservation time slots, fictional locations, and how the interface should work on mobile. At the same time, the AI constrained me because it could make technical and design decisions faster than I could verify them. For example, it added features such as search and availability filters during the first build even though I had not specifically requested them. I could judge whether these features were useful from a user perspective, but I could not confidently assess every technical choice behind them. The bottleneck therefore shifted from building the application to checking and understanding what the AI had actually built.

## Q3. In the loop

My judgment was genuinely needed when I corrected or rejected something the AI had produced. For example, I prompted, **“remove this component. change nothing else”**, because I decided that the introductory banner the AI had included was unnecessary for the homepage. My judgment also mattered when I asked it to **“make the app adjust accordingly in different device preview such that nothing spills out”**, because I could visually identify that the interface was not working properly across screen sizes. However, I was only nominally in the loop when I accepted some of the AI-generated features from the initial build, such as the search bar and availability filters, without fully questioning why they had been added. If this were a real reservation system, displaying validated reservation availability could eventually operate out of the loop, unusual availability patterns could be monitored on the loop, while consequential actions such as confirming, cancelling, or changing a customer's reservation should remain in the loop. This build showed me that human approval only provides meaningful control when the person understands what changed and is able to challenge it.

## Q4. What did it build?

The most important thing the AI built that I never explicitly specified was additional behaviour around the basic two-screen reservation tracker. My original request was mainly for a homepage showing cafe outlets and a second screen showing reservation availability, but the AI independently added features such as a search bar, availability filters, a live counter, reservation status indicators and other interface decisions. These appeared reasonable when I first saw them, so they quickly became part of the product even though I had never asked for them. During later prompts, I became more specific by repeatedly using instructions such as **“change nothing else”** to limit unintended changes. To catch this earlier, I should have reviewed every iteration by asking three questions: what did I request, what did the AI add that I did not request, and whether I actually wanted those additions. The key lesson was that anything I leave unspecified can still become a product decision because the AI will often fill in the gaps itself.

## Q5. Learning pointers

My build suggests three actions for an organisation using these tools at scale. First, AI-generated applications should still have a clearly identified owner who reviews what the AI has added, because even my simple cafe reservation tracker gained features and behaviours beyond my original requirements. Second, organisations should establish clear prompting and review standards, including requiring developers or business users to identify changes that were not explicitly requested, because AI-generated defaults can quickly become accepted product behaviour. Third, the prompt history and major design decisions should remain with the application so another employee can understand why certain features were added or changed. My experience showed that AI can dramatically reduce the effort required to build a working application, but it does not remove the need for human judgment, verification and governance.
